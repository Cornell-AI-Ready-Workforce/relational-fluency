## Scenario references

### 1. The missed deadlines — Perspective taking

1. **Eyal, T., Steffel, M., & Epley, N. (2018).** "Perspective Mistaking: Accurately Understanding the Mind of Another Requires Getting Perspective, Not Taking Perspective." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 114(4), 547–571. <https://www.nicholasepley.com/publications>
   *Relevance:* Imagining the IC's situation is unreliable; the manager must elicit perspective rather than assume.
2. **Ku, G., Wang, C. S., & Galinsky, A. D. (2015).** "The Promise and Perversity of Perspective-Taking in Organizations." *Research in Organizational Behavior*, 35, 79–102. <https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0191308515000040>
   *Relevance:* When perspective-taking helps managers and when it backfires — useful for scoring whether the manager probes effectively or imposes their narrative.
3. **Gallup.** *State of the Global Workplace* (annual). <https://www.gallup.com/workplace/349484/state-of-the-global-workplace.aspx>
   *Relevance:* Documents widespread disengagement / life-affecting factors at work — i.e., the realistic base rate that an unexplained performance dip often has personal causes worth probing.

### 2. Pushback in the meeting — Perspective taking

1. **Galinsky, A. D., Maddux, W. W., Gilin, D., & White, J. B. (2008).** *Psychological Science*, 19(4), 378–384. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18399891/>
   *Relevance:* Cognitive perspective-taking surfaces the *real interest* behind a position — here, "being blindsided" not "the proposal is bad."
2. **Stone, D., Patton, B., & Heen, S. (1999/2010).** *Difficult Conversations: How to Discuss What Matters Most.* Penguin/Viking. <https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/722989/difficult-conversations-by-douglas-stone-bruce-patton-and-sheila-heen-foreword-by-roger-fisher/>
   *Relevance:* The "What happened? / Feelings / Identity" framework separates surface disagreement from the actual grievance.

### 3. Unexpected critical feedback — Emotional regulation

1. **Gross, J. J. (2015).** *Psychological Inquiry*, 26(1), 1–26. <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781>
   *Relevance:* Reappraisal vs. suppression maps to the scoring: cognitive reappraisal (curiosity), not expressive suppression (white-knuckling) or response amplification (defending/over-apologizing).
2. **Stone, D., & Heen, S. (2014).** *Thanks for the Feedback: The Science and Art of Receiving Feedback Well.* Penguin/Viking. <https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/313485/thanks-for-the-feedback-by-douglas-stone-and-sheila-heen/>
   *Relevance:* Names the three feedback triggers (truth/relationship/identity) driving defensiveness, and the "receive-then-evaluate" practice the scenario rewards.
3. **Dweck, C. (2016).** "What Having a 'Growth Mindset' Actually Means." *HBR*. <https://hbr.org/2016/01/what-having-a-growth-mindset-actually-means>
   *Relevance:* Distinguishes a real growth-mindset response from the "false growth mindset" performance (nodding/over-apologizing) the scenario should *not* reward.

### 4. Credit taken in front of leadership — Emotional regulation

1. **David, S., & Congleton, C. (2013).** "Emotional Agility." *HBR* (Nov 2013). <https://hbr.org/2013/11/emotional-agility>
   *Relevance:* "Show up, step out, walk your why" — grammar for being angry-but-effective: feel the anger, label it, act on the underlying value (fairness/credit) rather than from the affect.
2. **Gross, J. J. (2015).** *Psychological Inquiry*, 26(1), 1–26. <https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781>
   *Relevance:* Identification → selection → implementation stages give a scoring backbone for whether the user *uses* the anger as information vs. dumps or buries it.

### 5. The Slack snap — Apology and repair

1. **Lewicki, R. J., Polin, B., & Lount, R. B. (2016).** *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research*, 9(2), 177–196. <https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/264/>
   *Relevance:* Six-component apology structure (acknowledgment of responsibility ranked highest; offer of repair second) is a direct rubric.
2. **Schumann, K. (2018).** "The Psychology of Offering an Apology: Understanding the Barriers to Apologizing and How to Overcome Them." *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 27(2), 74–78. <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0963721417741709>
   *Relevance:* The three barriers (self-image threat, low concern for victim, perceived ineffectiveness) produce the exact failure modes the scenario stresses.
3. **Lazare, A. (2004).** *On Apology.* Oxford University Press. <https://global.oup.com/academic/product/on-apology-9780195189117>
   *Relevance:* Canonical taxonomy of failed apologies (vague, conditional, "I'm sorry if…") — the patterns to catch.

### 6. The shipped bad decision — Apology and repair

1. **Edmondson, A. C. (2023).** "It's OK to Fail, but You Have to Do It Right." *HBR* (July 28, 2023). <https://hbr.org/2023/07/its-ok-to-fail-but-you-have-to-do-it-right>
   *Relevance:* Frames the leader's job after a bad call: own it without conflating intelligent-risk failure with negligence, and protect the team's willingness to keep taking risks.
2. **Lewicki, R. J., Polin, B., & Lount, R. B. (2016).** *Negotiation and Conflict Management Research*, 9(2), 177–196. <https://ncmr.lps.library.cmu.edu/article/id/264/>
   *Relevance:* "Acknowledgment of responsibility" + "offer of repair" are the two highest-impact components — what real ownership in the debrief looks like.
3. **Edmondson, A. C., & Bransby, D. P. (2023).** "Psychological Safety Comes of Age: Observed Themes in an Established Literature." *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*, 10, 55–78. <https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-120920-055217>
   *Relevance:* How leader fallibility-modeling shapes whether the team will speak up after the next bad call — the second-order outcome the debrief is really for.

### 7. The quiet report — Psychological safety

1. **Edmondson, A. C. (1999).** *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(2), 350–383. <https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2307/2666999>
   *Relevance:* Defines psych safety as felt safety for interpersonal risk-taking — the construct measured when the manager tries to make it safe for the IC to surface a real view.
2. **Google re:Work.** "Understand Team Effectiveness" (Project Aristotle). <https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness>
   *Relevance:* The practitioner-facing manager moves (framing work as learning, modeling curiosity, acknowledging fallibility) are the concrete behaviors to display.
3. **Edmondson, A. C., & Bransby, D. P. (2023).** *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior*, 10, 55–78. <https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-120920-055217>
   *Relevance:* Specific inquiry behaviors that *invite* voice without coercing it — i.e., not making the invitation itself feel like pressure.

### 8. The burned-out team — Psychological safety

1. **Edmondson, A. C. (2003).** "Speaking Up in the Operating Room: How Team Leaders Promote Learning in Interdisciplinary Action Teams." *Journal of Management Studies*, 40(6), 1419–1452. <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-6486.00386>
   *Relevance:* Team leaders earn disclosure through observable in-the-moment coaching behaviors — directly relevant to a new lead earning the senior IC's honest take in one conversation.
2. **Maslach, C., Schaufeli, W. B., & Leiter, M. P. (2001).** "Job Burnout." *Annual Review of Psychology*, 52, 397–422. <https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.psych.52.1.397>
   *Relevance:* Exhaustion / cynicism / inefficacy triad and the six worklife mismatches give the lead a diagnostic vocabulary for what "performative docs + burnout" actually signals.
3. **Microsoft.** *Work Trend Index Annual Report.* <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index>
   *Relevance:* Documents the "infinite workday" / interruption / overwhelm pattern that produces the performative-docs-and-burnout dynamic.

## Workplace incivility (for the researcher's incivility dial)

Grounds the v2 incivility knobs (condescension, sarcasm, dismissiveness,
passive aggression). Each knob ramps from 0 (no fragment injected) through a
mid band ("occasional, possibly unintentional") to a high band ("consistent,
deniable, designed to make the target feel small without a single
pointable line"). That deniability is exactly Andersson & Pearson's defining
feature.

- **Andersson, L. M., & Pearson, C. M. (1999).** "Tit for Tat? The Spiraling Effect of Incivility in the Workplace." *Academy of Management Review*, 24(3), 452–471. <https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.1999.2202131>
  *Relevance:* Foundational definition of workplace incivility as "low-intensity deviant behavior with ambiguous intent to harm the target, in violation of workplace norms for mutual respect." The ambiguity/deniability framing drives the design of the high-band knob fragments.
- **Cortina, L. M., Magley, V. J., Williams, J. H., & Langhout, R. D. (2001).** "Incivility in the Workplace: Incidence and Impact." *Journal of Occupational Health Psychology*, 6(1), 64–80. <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11199258/>
  *Relevance:* The original behavioral taxonomy and Workplace Incivility Scale (WIS) — the specific behaviors (interrupting, ignoring, talking down, dismissive comments) the knobs operationalize.
- **Cortina, L. M. (2008).** "Unseen Injustice: Incivility as Modern Discrimination in Organizations." *Academy of Management Review*, 33(1), 55–75. <https://doi.org/10.5465/amr.2008.27745097>
  *Relevance:* Argues that incivility often functions as a covert channel for bias against marginalized employees — important framing if you analyze incivility-by-target-identity across sessions.
- **Schilpzand, P., De Pater, I. E., & Erez, A. (2016).** "Workplace incivility: A review of the literature and agenda for future research." *Journal of Organizational Behavior*, 37(S1), S57–S88. <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/job.1976>
  *Relevance:* Comprehensive review — useful when defending scenario design choices to reviewers.
- **Porath, C., & Pearson, C. (2013).** "The Price of Incivility." *HBR* (Jan–Feb 2013). <https://hbr.org/2013/01/the-price-of-incivility>
  *Relevance:* Industry-facing translation; useful citation for non-academic audiences and for explaining why this dial matters operationally.
